Kilambi Research Working Paper No. KR-WP-2026-39

The Learning-Curve Treadmill: Wright's Law, Capacity Reinvestment, and Downturn Risk in South Korea and Taiwan

Kilambi Research

November 2026

Download PDF

Abstract

South Korea and Taiwan hold the two most concentrated semiconductor positions in the world economy. In October 2026 semiconductors were 49.0 percent of Korean exports (Reconstructed, B), and Samsung Electronics with SK hynix were 42.2 percent of KOSPI capitalisation in May 2026 (Observed, B). TSMC was about 43 percent of the TAIEX in November 2025 (Observed, B). This paper asks whether reinvesting boom surpluses into capacity on steep learning curves amplifies the damage when demand pauses. Three computed results frame the answer. First, the DRAM learning rate estimated in KR-WP-2026-05 at 38.6 percent reproduces exactly on the parent data and steepens across subperiods, from 35.1 percent in 1971–1990 to 47.4 percent in 1991–2007 (Reconstructed, B), while TSMC revenue per wafer rises with cumulative output, an apparent learning rate of −34.0 percent (Reconstructed, C): the treadmill is a memory phenomenon, not a foundry phenomenon, in the data held. Second, in the four downturn episodes since 2000, Korean chip exports fell 19 to 28 percent and Samsung group operating profit fell 39 to 85 percent, while TSMC revenue fell at most 24 percent and rose 4.5 percent in 2019. Third, at the fitted DRAM curve, bit growth of 20 percent per year implies a price decline of 12.0 percent per year, so volume must grow 13.7 percent per year to hold revenue flat; a representative new fab breaks even only if prices fall less than 37.0 percent at full utilisation and less than 29.3 percent at 70 percent utilisation (Modelled, C), against observed episode price falls of 43 to 59 percent. Applying the 2022–23 episode rates to the 2025 base puts Korean semiconductor exports US$41.1 billion lower and SK hynix at an operating loss (Modelled, B/C). The thesis holds for commodity memory and fails, so far, for contracted HBM and leading-edge foundry output. The loss order in a crash runs from memory-firm equity to Korean policy lenders; TSMC enters with operating cash flow above capital expenditure and no policy-loan channel of comparable size.
