Working Paper 30

What the Meter Keeps: Interval Data Practices for Large Electricity Customers, and the Statistical Limits of Detecting Small Savings

Kilambi Research

October 2026

Download PDF

Abstract

This paper asks two questions that are usually treated separately. First, what electricity consumption data do utilities actually collect, retain, and make available for large commercial and industrial (C&I) customers? We document practices across 57 jurisdictions in North America, Europe, and the rest of the world, separating five layers that public discussion routinely conflates: the meter’s measurement capability, the recorded interval, the transmission cadence, the resolution and history retained by the utility, and the data a customer or authorised third party can obtain. Second, given the data that realistically exists, how small a change in whole-building electricity consumption can modern statistical methods detect and quantify? Using the portfolio evidence on baseline-model accuracy and the ASHRAE Guideline 14 fractional-savings-uncertainty framework, we compute detection and quantification limits for savings of 2, 3, 5, 8, and 10 percent.

Three findings stand out. Recording is rarely the binding constraint: large C&I meters record at 15 minutes across most of the European Union and much of North America, at 30 minutes across much of Asia-Pacific, and at 5 minutes in a small set of markets. Retention and access are the binding constraints: customer-obtainable history clusters at 12 to 24 months where it is documented at all, and utility retention schedules are unpublished in most of the jurisdictions studied. Statistically, daily or hourly interval data are sufficient to quantify savings of about 5 percent at a typical well-modelled site, and about 3 percent at 68 percent confidence; savings of 2 percent are detectable but generally not quantifiable at an arbitrary site; and monthly billing data cannot support any of these claims. Finer-than-hourly data adds little detection power once residual autocorrelation is counted, because the binding constraint is unexplained operational variance at the scale of days to seasons, not sampling density.
