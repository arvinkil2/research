  1. Working Paper 08

    Time to Power: The Financing Cost of Energy-Delayed Megaprojects

    Arvin Kilambi

    September 2026

    PDF

    We assemble an audited dataset on 19 large capital-intensive projects (19 rows, 6 sectors: data centers, semiconductors, mining, LNG, battery manufacturing, industrial processing), nine of which were materially affected by energy-infrastructure constraints, and ask when the cost of carrying financing obligations on a delayed asset exceeds the cost of directly funding the missing power. A 12-mechanism delay-cost model, applied to a stylized 500 MW / $6.0B data center, puts a 12-month energy-driven delay at $857.6M in present value (14.3% of capex, $71.5M per month); the timing value of delayed revenue is the largest term at every horizon (about two-fifths of the total), while financing-only terms are 35% of the 12-month total, so counting only interest during delay understates cost by roughly two-thirds. Five independent energy supply-chain links each run 30 months or more in typical lead time (greenfield transmission 120 months, generator interconnection queues 55 months median, gas-turbine slots 5-7 years quoted, large transformers 30 months average with 48-60 month Tier-1 quotes, large-load interconnection 36 months typical). A wait-vs-solve model over 3-, 6-, 12-, 24-, and 36-month delays finds that no permanent alternative beats waiting at 12 months or less; reciprocating gas engines are the only alternative that beats waiting inside 60 months, from roughly 24-28 months of expected delay, supporting a rational capex premium up to $3,049/kW at 36 months (against $1,600/kW market capex) and a delivered-energy ceiling near $78/MWh. The data support a durable but narrow business thesis: willingness to pay for time-to-power is large (order $140/kW per month of acceleration) yet confined to delays beyond roughly two years, alternatives priced near grid power, and revenue-intense projects. We state the dashboard readings that would prove this wrong. All figures are nominal USD, base year 2026, unless stated.

  2. Working Paper 07

    Finite-term IP rights as an institutional investment thesis: evidence, pricing, and the central test

    Arvin Kilambi

    September 2026

    PDF

    This paper tests whether a scalable institutional investment thesis exists around acquiring or financing finite-term (5–10 year) economic and commercialization rights in music IP, and whether the economics generalize to other royalty-producing assets. The thesis, as stated: underwrite 8–12% asset-level cash yields plus 5–10 percentage points of active commercialization uplift to earn 18–25% gross IRRs, without viral outcomes, aggressive leverage, or speculative terminal values. The paper assembles the evidence first: 50 sourced transactions, listed-fund realized returns, royalty decay and survivorship data, documented operating alpha, comparable structures, cross-asset evidence, legal feasibility across six jurisdictions, and failure cases. A deterministic cash-flow model (245 scenarios) then runs the central test. The conclusion is a single outcome of a three-outcome design: the evidence does not support the stated thesis, and the narrower disciplined-pricing variant does not survive either. The thesis is rejected.

  3. Working Paper 06

    Powering the boom: AI datacenter capex, compute efficiency, and the oversupply question

    Arvin Kilambi

    September 2026

    PDF

    Hyperscaler cash capital expenditure grew 31.6% per year from $23.0 billion in 2015 to $357.5 billion in 2025. Staged 2030 US supply of 87–152 GW compares with bear, base, and bull demand of 56, 96, and 132 GW, while the current market records 1.4% vacancy.

  4. Working Paper 05

    A century of falling costs? Wright’s law, commodity prices, and the limits of learning-by-doing, 1925–2026

    Arvin Kilambi

    September 2026

    PDF

    Estimated learning rates are 38.6% for DRAM, 32.5% for computers, and 27.7% for solar photovoltaic modules. The median real cost trend is −4.05% per year across 31 manufactured-goods series, compared with +0.51% across 50 deflated commodity-price series.

  5. Working Paper 04

    How the largest economies fund debt service

    Arvin Kilambi

    September 2026

    PDF

    US central-government interest expenditure rose from 10.9% of revenue in 2020 to 20.3% in 2024, while India’s 2022 burden was 34%. Refinancing represented about 93% of US gross issuance in 2024 and 83% of Japan’s FY2025 issuance.

  6. Working Paper 03

    Sovereign bond markets, 2016–2026

    Arvin Kilambi

    September 2026

    PDF

    Major-market 10-year yields rose by about 3 percentage points from their 2019–2020 troughs. Approximately 80% of OECD gross sovereign borrowing in 2025 refinanced maturing debt.

  7. Working Paper 02

    The finite horizon: variance dominance, the description–inference distinction, and why published odds describe the process, not the participant

    Arvin Kilambi

    September 2026

    PDF

    Across seven processes, the wrong-side outcome rate approaches a common asymptotic curve when sample size is normalized by the variance horizon. A closed result such as 5/10 is exact as description; uncertainty enters when it is used to infer a broader process.

  8. Working Paper 01

    Fuel composition as a dynamic control variable in spark-ignition engines: chemistry, operating regimes, and the limits of blend scheduling

    Arvin Kilambi

    September 2026

    PDF

    Stoichiometric blend scheduling adds 0.015 efficiency points and ambient-adaptive selection adds 0.05 points. Lean load-adaptive switching between hydrogen and a hydrogen–methane blend adds 0.95 points, or 1.44 points under drive-weighting, over the best fixed fuel.
