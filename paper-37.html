Kilambi Research Working Paper No. KR-WP-2026-37

Who Pays When the Wholesale Price Moves? Contracting, Gap Charges and Pass-Through in World Electricity Markets

Kilambi Research

November 2026

Download PDF

Abstract

When a wholesale electricity price falls, generators are often assumed to lose and customers to gain. In most of the world's electricity systems that assumption fails, because most electricity is not sold at the wholesale price. It is sold under private contracts, under publicly backed contracts that settle the difference between a contract price and the market price, or under regulated tariffs that recover costs with a lag. This paper maps, market by market for the largest electricity systems in the world, how generators are paid, who absorbs the difference when market prices move, and how much of a price change reaches household and large-user bills. A computed worked example traces a $20/MWh price decline and increase through a stylized market: the average consumer cost moves only $6.00/MWh in either direction in year one, while the gap levy that funds contracted generation rises $5.00/MWh in the decline and turns negative in the increase. Market sections cover China, the United States by market design, the European Union through five member states and the 2024 rule mandating two-way contracts for difference, India, Japan, Brazil, South Korea, Canada with Ontario's Global Adjustment as the reference case, the United Kingdom, Australia, the Nordic market, South Africa, Mexico and the Philippines. Prevalence is estimated from covered volumes rather than whole-country classification. Against 2024 world electricity consumption of 30,856 TWh, regulated tariffs settle 34.8 percent, private contracts 21.1 percent, spot and merchant sales 9.0 percent, and publicly backed contracts 2.1 percent; the publicly backed volume is also exposed to spot settlement (an overlap of 2.1 percent of world consumption), and 33.1 percent of world consumption cannot be classified from published covered volumes.
